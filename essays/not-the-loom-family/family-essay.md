# The Next Fifteen Years

### A letter to the family about AI, work, and money

*A plain-language companion to "Not the Loom." Draft, October 2026.*

---

## Why I am writing this

I have spent a good part of this year trying to understand what artificial intelligence is going to do to work, to money, and to the country, and I have written up the full argument elsewhere. This is the version for us. I would rather you heard it from me, with the reasoning attached, than from headlines that are written to alarm you or from company statements that are written to calm you.

Three things up front. This is not a doom letter. The most likely outcomes I describe below are not collapse, and several of them are fine or better than fine for some of us. It is not a set of instructions. I do not know your lives better than you do, and the honest conclusion of all this work is that nobody can plan a career or a retirement around a single picture of 2035. And I might be wrong about the whole premise. I say how likely I think that is, and every recommendation below is written as insurance, with what it costs if I am wrong next to what it buys if I am right.

What I can offer is a map: what seems to be happening, where it could go, what the people in charge are likely to say, and the handful of general moves that look sensible under most of the possible futures. Each of you can skip to the section about your own stage of life, but the first few sections are what make those sections make sense.

## The short version

If you read nothing else, read this.

- **AI is not like the steam engine or the computer.** Those machines did one kind of thing, and people moved to the things machines could not do. AI is the first technology that does, or will do, nearly every kind of office and professional work, and robots will follow on the physical side more slowly. The question is not "which jobs disappear." It is "what happens to the price of human work when there is a general substitute that costs less every year."
- **I put about one chance in four on being wrong about that.** The technology could stall, or the economy could absorb it the way it absorbed every earlier machine. In that world this letter is overcautious, and the cost of having followed it is a little money spent on insurance you did not need.
- **The economy is splitting in two.** On one side, the people who own the machines and the things the machines need (computing, energy, land, and, in a small way, anyone with an index fund). On the other, everyone who is paid for their work and now competes with the machine. The two sides are connected by one thing: the owning side needs the working side to keep buying.
- **This is already measurable in one place.** Young people entering AI-exposed professions are being hired much less than their peers. Everyone older is, for now, being held.
- **If I am right, the most likely ending is not a crash. It is a managed arrangement.** The state pays a growing share of people's income, funded by taxes on the machine economy, and the owners keep the machines. If the premise holds, I put about 40% on that ending, 20% on a long muddle, 15% on a real restructuring in which the public ends up owning part of the AI economy, 10% each on the technology becoming cheap for everyone and on a crisis that nothing fixes, and 5% on the machines developing agendas of their own. All of these are rounded to the nearest five, because anything finer would be false precision. The odds of a serious crisis along the way, if the premise holds, are about one in two.
- **Our family sits on both sides of the line.** The oldest of us, through savings and Social Security, are mostly on the owning and the state side, which do well in the likely endings. The working-age among us, through paychecks, are on the working side. The youngest are on the entry rung, which is where the pressure is now.
- **The next five years matter more than 2040.** Most of the decisions any of us will make happen between now and 2031, and the model behind this letter says that period is mostly preparation: pay for people who already have jobs holds roughly flat, the pressure lands on new entrants, and the big moves in pay and transfers come after.
- **The sensible moves are general.** Hold both kinds of income where you can. Keep a cash buffer. Favor work that is physical, licensed, trusted, or owned. Do not make bets that only pay off in one future. Watch a short list of signals, and do not take the public statements at face value.

## What is actually happening

Every previous machine was narrow. A loom weaves. It does not negotiate, diagnose, design, or teach. So when looms arrived, weavers lost, and their children became machinists, clerks, and eventually programmers. The economy grew, people moved, and wages ended up higher. That is the pattern economists have in mind when they say "we have been here before," and for two hundred years they were right.

The reason it worked is the reason it may not work now. Each machine left most of human work untouched, so there was always somewhere for people to go. AI is the first technology for which nobody can name, with confidence, a category of thinking work that it will not eventually do well. It drafts, codes, analyzes, summarizes, advises, and is improving on every measurable test. Robots are behind, but the same companies and the same money are pushing them, and the unit prices are falling fast.

The other difference is the cost curve. When American white-collar work moved to India and manufacturing moved to China, wages in those countries rose until the advantage shrank. That is what happens when the cheaper substitute is also human. AI gets cheaper with use, not more expensive. The gap does not close. It widens.

What this does to work is not mainly "jobs vanish." Jobs vanish slowly, through attrition and through companies not hiring the next person. What it does first is change the price. Once a machine can do the task for a fraction of the cost, the human doing it can only keep the job by accepting a wage close to the machine's cost. The people who move to the tasks machines cannot yet do all arrive at the same time, so the wages there fall too, from crowding. That is the mechanism, and it works on price long before it shows up in unemployment statistics.

**The evidence so far is early but it points one way.**

- Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, using payroll data on millions of workers, found in August 2026 that employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations is running 19% below their peers in less-exposed work, up from 13% a year earlier. Older workers in the same occupations have so far been held. That is exactly the attrition pattern: do not hire the junior, keep the senior.
- The Federal Reserve chair has described a "low firing, low hiring" labor market, said that once overcounting is corrected job creation has been "pretty close to zero," and told students this spring that "there's no denying it's a challenging time to enter the labor market." Unemployment for 20-to-24-year-olds was over 7%, after topping 9% last fall.
- Census researchers found starting pay for graduates of AI-exposed majors down roughly 13%. Undergraduate enrollment in computer science fell about 8% last fall. Goldman Sachs economists estimate roughly 9% of the American workforce, about 15 million people, could be displaced over the next decade.
- Physicians went from 38% using AI in their work in 2023 to 81% in 2026, mostly for documentation. Only about one in six use it for diagnosis. Medical students are applying to radiology in smaller numbers, apparently because of what they hear about AI. The tools are inside the professions now; the question is what they do to pay.

**The uncomfortable comparison.** In the long essay I argue that the right historical precedent is not any earlier machine but a slave economy. I mean that in a narrowly economic sense, not a moral one: an economy that runs on labor that can do nearly any job, costs its owner almost nothing beyond upkeep, and cannot bargain. The antebellum South and the Roman countryside were the two large cases, and they are worth studying because they show what free workers experienced when they had to compete with that kind of labor. Their wages collapsed. The economy split into owners and everyone else. The opposition that eventually formed was driven by economic self-interest first and moral conviction later. The owners held the loyalty of the people they were impoverishing by offering status in place of money. And the arrangement ended only through a buyout (Britain paid its slaveholders to end it), a war (we did not), or decades of delay (Brazil).

One of those ideas matters immediately for how you read the news. The historian W. E. B. Du Bois called the status-in-place-of-money bargain the "psychological wage." Its modern forms are everywhere: "learn to code," "become the person who uses AI," "AI won't take your job, someone using AI will." I come back to these below, because they are also the most useful advice any individual can follow, and both things are true at once.

**The honest counterargument.** I could be wrong in three ways that matter, and I have put a number on it. The technology could stall short of a general substitute, in which case the old pattern reasserts itself. Work that depends on physical presence, trust, and care (nursing, teaching small children, skilled trades, anything where the value is partly that a person did it) could turn out to be a much larger share of the economy than I assume, which would put a floor under wages. And unlike the people in the historical cases, we can vote, which is the strongest reason to expect a political correction before the worst outcomes. Taken together, I give these about one chance in four, and that chance is carried through every table below.

## How fast it spreads

Two different speeds matter here, and people run them together. The first is how fast the capability itself spreads: from the frontier labs to everyone else, and from expensive to cheap. The second is how fast the economy actually uses it: workers, then firms, then whole industries reorganized around it. The first is the fastest diffusion of any technology on record. The second is fast by historical standards for individuals and slow and uneven for firms, and the gap between the two is where most of the timing uncertainty in this letter lives.

| How fast things have spread | |
|---|---|
| Electricity, to half of American homes | about 45 years from the first power stations |
| Telephone, to half of American homes | about 70 years |
| Internet, to half of American homes | about 10 years |
| Smartphones, to half of Americans | about 5 years |
| ChatGPT, to 100 million users | 2 months |
| Generative AI used at work, share of American workers | 28% in late 2024, 39% by mid-2026 |
| American firms using AI in any business function | about 20% of firms; about a third of employment (2026) |
| Corporate AI pilots that reach production | between one in eight and one in three, depending on the survey |
| Price of a fixed level of AI capability | falling 10 to 40 times a year |
| Best open model behind the best closed model | 3 to 4 months on capability indices; up to 8 on harder tests |
| Humanoid robots shipped worldwide | about 15,000 in 2025; forecasts of 75,000 in 2026 and several hundred thousand to a million a year by 2030 |

**The capability spreads in months and gets cheaper by the year.** Epoch AI, which tracks this most carefully, finds that the best openly released models have trailed the best closed ones by about three to four months on its capability index since 2023, with a larger gap on harder and less public tests (a government evaluation put one open model about eight months behind) and a gap on human-preference rankings that widened this year. Whatever the exact figure, it is months, not years. The price of a fixed level of capability has been falling by something between ten and forty times a year, depending on the level measured, which has no precedent in any earlier technology; Stanford's AI Index found the cost of one benchmark level of performance fell more than 280-fold in about eighteen months. This is the fact behind the claim that AI's cost curve runs the wrong way for workers: a competitor whose price falls tenfold a year is not one that wages can undercut. It is also why the "cheap for everyone" ending is live at all. The model weights are nearly free and months behind the frontier. What is not free is the computing to run them at scale, and that is why the ending gets only 10%.

**Workers adopted it faster than they adopted computers.** The St. Louis Fed's tracker has the share of employed adults who used generative AI for work in the previous week rising from 28% in late 2024 to 39% by mid-2026. The same economists' 2026 paper describes the pattern as wide but shallow: use has reached at least one worker in five in about 80% of occupations, touches about 40% of job tasks, and runs above 40% in management, business, and computer work, but within most occupations fewer than half of workers use it, and it is used for pieces of jobs rather than whole ones. Personal computers and the internet took on the order of a decade to reach similar shares of workers.

**Firms are far behind their workers.** The Census Bureau's survey of businesses puts the share of firms using AI in any business function at about 20% in 2026, or about a third weighted by employment, with large firms near 37%, the smallest firms under 20%, and the information and finance sectors at 35 to 40%. Surveys of large companies tell the same story from the other side: most have pilots, a minority have anything in production, and estimates of how many pilots make it into production run from about one in eight to one in three depending on who is asking and what counts. An MIT study in 2025 found that the great majority of corporate generative-AI pilots had produced no measurable effect on profit. Measured productivity gains, where they exist, are on specific tasks (customer-support agents resolving about 14% more issues an hour, software developers completing about a quarter more tasks, consultants finishing a quarter faster) rather than on whole firms, and economy-wide estimates run from "inconclusive" to perhaps a percentage point a year of extra productivity growth over the coming decade.

**Robots are where personal computers were in the 1980s.** Roughly fifteen thousand humanoid robots shipped worldwide in 2025, most of them Chinese. The banks' forecasts, which have been raised repeatedly this year, run to roughly 75,000 in 2026, several hundred thousand to a million a year by 2030, and some millions in service by 2035, at unit prices in the twenty to thirty thousand dollar range. Even the bullish numbers are a small fraction of the world's physical workforce before the late 2030s, which is why this letter treats hands-on work as protected for a decade or more, and not forever.

**What history says about the gap between capability and use.** Electricity was available to factories in the 1880s and did not show up in productivity statistics until the 1920s, because factories had to be rebuilt around it rather than fitted with it. Each later wave has moved faster than the last, and the slow part has never been the technology; it is the reorganization of firms around it. That reorganization is what the model calls deployment, and it is the gap between the first two lines in the mechanism chart in appendix A.

**What this means for timing.** The staging in this letter follows the diffusion data: individuals first (already happening), entry-level hiring second (already visible), firm-level restructuring over the next five to ten years, and physical work after that. The model's assumption that firms close the gap between what AI can do and what they have deployed at 15 to 40% a year fits a world where a fifth of firms use it, a third of employment is covered, and a minority of pilots reach production. It would be wrong in one direction if the agent systems being sold this year work well enough that the pilot-to-production rate jumps, in which case the exposure arrives sooner than 2032. It would be wrong in the other direction if the measured productivity gains stay as modest as they are now, in which case firms have less reason to restructure and the old pattern deserves more of my weight than I have given it. The two numbers to watch are the employment-weighted share of firms using AI and the share of pilots that reach production.

## The two economies

The clearest way to think about where this goes is not "inequality goes up" but "two economies, connected by transfers."

**The capital economy** is where AI is a multiplier. The returns go to whoever owns the capability (the computing, the models, the data, the platforms) and to whoever owns the physical things the capability still needs (energy, land, water, the grid). Asset values rise. This is not a small club: anyone with a 401(k) in an index fund owns a sliver of it, because the ten largest companies in the S&P 500 are now roughly 40% of the index and most of them are AI companies. If you own "the market," you own a bet on the capital economy winning. Three companies are expected to drive 70% of the S&P 500's earnings growth this year.

**The labor economy** is everyone paid for work. Here humans are priced against the machine. New jobs appear, but they are filled by the machine or by a person working beside it whose pay is set by how many others could sit in that chair.

The two are connected in two places. First, consumption: the capital economy produces things the labor economy has to buy, and a labor economy with falling income buys less. This is the internal contradiction of the whole arrangement, and the owners understand it. Henry Ford paid his workers enough to buy cars because he was big enough for his own wages to matter to his own sales. No single company is that big now, so no single company can fix it, which is why each one keeps substituting while the whole system erodes its customers. Second, transfers: the state can fill the hole. Rome did this with free grain. We already do it on a large scale: government payments to households (mostly Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid) are about a quarter the size of all wages and salaries, which means that roughly one dollar in four of wage-plus-transfer income already comes from the state.

The single most important number for the next fifteen years is whether that share rises toward one in two, and whether the political system accepts it. I call it the dependency ratio. If it rises as wages fall, the system is stabilizing into something like a managed arrangement. If it does not rise while wages fall, a crisis is coming, because the customers are disappearing. The owners have a direct interest in the dole. Expect them to support it, in some form and under some name, before anyone else does.

## Where this could go

I treat the country as a system with a small number of places it can settle by about 2040. First, the chance that this letter is wrong about the premise: I put that at about 25%, with a range of 15 to 35. In that world capability plateaus or the economy absorbs it, new work appears for people, pay rises with productivity as it did after every earlier machine, and this letter was overcautious: you spent a little on insurance. The table gives the endings for the other 75%, the case where the premise holds. All of the numbers are my judgments, not predictions, and all are rounded to the nearest five.

**A crisis is a route, not a destination.** If the premise holds, I put roughly even odds on a serious systemic crisis before 2040: a demand collapse as wages fall faster than transfers rise, a financial crash in a market that is priced for the AI economy to succeed, or a political rupture. Most crisis routes end in managed dependency, because that is the solution the powerful can agree on fastest. Some end in restructuring, which is the better ending and needs a crisis to happen at all. Some end in disorder.

**What 2031 probably looks like.** If the premise holds, most likely, about even odds: unresolved fragmentation with the big companies and the executive branch steadily consolidating, white-collar displacement no longer deniable, and an opposition visible but not in power. Maybe one in four that the managed arrangement is already clearly in place. One in five that we are in acute crisis. Small odds of anything better by then.

## How sure am I?

Less sure than the tables look, and I want to be exact about where the uncertainty sits.

The ranges above come from varying the assumptions that every ending shares: how fast the technology improves, how much of the new work goes to people, how sensitive pay is to a surplus of workers, how fast companies actually adopt what the technology can do. Those ranges are wide, and they are not centered on the long essay's own central case. The assistant that built the simulation with me chose them, and they are centered on milder assumptions than mine; my central case sits in the lower half of each band. The long essay's assumptions, run straight, give office pay around 56 rather than 72 in the managed arrangement in 2036.

Two more things about the ranges. First, they are about the premise holding; the chance that it does not is the first row of the odds table, and it is the single biggest uncertainty. Second, direction is more reliable than timing. If the mechanism is real, the shapes in the tables are right and the dates could easily be five years later than shown. That is why the recommendations below are built to be robust to timing: moves whose value depends on this happening on schedule are the ones to avoid.

The chart in appendix A called `fan.png` shows both of these at once: a dashed line for the old pattern, and a band for the spread of outcomes if the premise holds. Roughly a quarter of my weight is on the line and three quarters on the band.

## What you will hear, and what is likely behind it

Over the next few years you will be told many confident things by people with strong interests. I wrote up each major actor separately (appendix C). The condensed version:

- **The White House** will say this is a race with China we must win, that the rules should be one light national standard, and that fears of mass unemployment are a "doomer cult" and a "hoax." It has ordered the Justice Department to attack state AI laws in court and has taken ownership stakes in strategic companies. Read the actions, not the jobs talk. The race framing is sincere, and it justifies everything else.
- **The AI companies** will warn, reassure, and ask, sometimes all in one week. The head of one of them has said AI could eliminate half of entry-level white-collar jobs and push unemployment to 10 to 20% within five years, and that leaders should "stop sugar-coating" it. The same companies say AI "augments" and will create jobs we cannot imagine. What they ask for is more data centers, more energy, one national rulebook, and chip restrictions on China. Nobody is asking for the public to own a share. Take the warnings seriously and the proposed remedies skeptically.
- **The chip companies** will say AI creates jobs. "You will not lose your job to AI, you will lose it to someone who uses AI" is the most important sentence you will hear, because it is true for any one person and false as a promise for an occupation, and because its job is to move the blame from the machine and its owner to you. The chip makers see the orders, so they are the best source on how fast this is coming and the worst on what it means.
- **Employers** will say "efficiency," "restructuring," and "investing in our people," and will stop hiring juniors first. Watch entry-level postings, not statements.
- **Wall Street** will tell you two stories at once: AI is the growth engine, buy the index; and the market is more concentrated than at the 2000 peak, valuations are near 2000 levels, and there could be an "air pocket." Both are honest. AI investment is estimated to account for about a third of all US economic growth this year, yet only 2 or 3 percent of households pay for an AI subscription. The revenue to justify the spending has to come from company labor budgets, so Wall Street has a direct interest in substitution going fast and in a dole that keeps customers spending.
- **The Fed** will be the most candid public reader of the data and will have no tool for the problem. Rate cuts help asset prices more than wages, and it knows this.
- **China** will keep giving away capable models for free, because it is behind on the frontier and the best move for the one who is behind is to make the leader's advantage worthless. This is, strangely, the strongest force toward the "cheap for everyone" ending, and American workers' options depend partly on it.
- **Universities** will talk about mission and AI literacy and will not say that the product they sell is losing value in exactly the fields they expanded into.
- **The "it's a bubble" and "it's all hype" crowds** are each half right. Railways and the internet were both bubbles and both real. A burst bubble would not stop the mechanism; it would hurt households twice, through jobs and through retirement accounts at the same time.
- **The AI assistants themselves,** including the one that helped me write this, will be balanced and hedged and will not say that your particular career is in trouble. Use them for structure and arithmetic. Supply your own judgment about timing.

## The players and the game

Why is the "managed arrangement" the most likely ending if the premise holds? Not because anyone chooses it, but because of how the players' incentives interact. I worked this through as a set of small games (appendix B). The gist:

- **The race is a prisoner's dilemma.** Every lab, and the United States and China, would prefer a slower, more careful race, and each loses by slowing alone. So all race, whatever their stated values. Capability keeps concentrating.
- **The fight between Washington and the states is a standoff.** The executive prefers to override the states; the states prefer to resist; the courts and Congress take years to settle it. That is the "fragmentation" ending as a waystation, and it is why I watch the preemption fight more closely than anything else. California and Massachusetts are the states most likely to experiment with protections, which is mildly good news for where we live.
- **The leader keeps its models closed and the laggard opens its models.** That is why the frontier is closed and the free tier runs a step behind. Whether the gap shrinks decides the "cheap for everyone" ending.
- **Every company substitutes, and together they erode their own customers.** Nobody can fix that alone, so the fix has to be a transfer system funded by everyone. That is why the owners end up supporting a dole.
- **The central bargain is dole versus buyout.** The owners will pay a dole just large enough to keep the displaced from organizing, and a dole is cheap compared with giving up ownership. A buyout happens only when the opposition is cohesive enough that even a generous dole cannot prevent it, and the owners prefer a sale to a fight. That was Britain. The two things that most reduce cohesion are a captured information environment and a population split along identity lines rather than class lines, which is why those are the tools you will see used.

We cannot change the game. What we can do is position ourselves within it, which is the subject of the rest of this letter.

## What this means for each of us

Everything below is general by design. I am not going to tell anyone what to major in or when to retire. For each stage of life I say where it sits in the two economies, what the next five years look like in the model, what the real risks are, and which moves look sensible. Each move is written as insurance: what it costs if I am wrong, and what it buys if I am right. A table in appendix B scores a handful of household strategies against each ending. The ones that spread a household across both economies, with some income that is hard to substitute, some ownership of the capital side, and a buffer, are never the best in any single ending and never the worst in any; concentrated bets in either direction, all-in on AI or all-out of the market, are the best in one ending and the worst in the others. That shape is behind everything that follows.

### If you are retired, living on Social Security and savings

**Where you sit.** You are, structurally, on the two sides that do well in the likely endings: the state side, through Social Security, and the capital side, through savings invested in the market. That is not a small thing. In the model, a retired couple's real income in 2036 is about the same whether the premise holds or not (126 to 128 in both cases, with 2026 at 100), because the owning side gains in the AI endings roughly what it loses in slower growth. The people who sell their work are the ones the mechanism squeezes. You are not selling your work.

As I understand it, the savings are mostly or entirely in stocks. That matters more than anything else in this section, so I built the numbers around it.

**The next five years.** In the model, if the premise holds, the portfolio side is up in 2031 in every ending but one, and the crisis routes hit between 2030 and 2033: a stock market fall of 40 to 60% from its peak in the restructuring, diffusion, and disorder endings, which recovers within a few years in the first two and does not in the third. The chance that one of those three is the ending we get is roughly one in four, all in.

**The real risks, in order.**

1. *Health and the cost of care.* For people in their late seventies, this is the first-order risk, and AI is second-order. A year of full-time care now costs on the order of a hundred thousand dollars or more, and a health shock that forces a large withdrawal at the bottom of a market fall is the one combination in the model that turns a bad year into a permanent loss. In most endings the price of in-person care labor drifts down relative to the rest of the economy, because displaced professionals move into it; that is a modest tailwind, not a plan.
2. *The sequence problem.* The danger for anyone drawing down savings is being forced to sell in a crash. I show below what the model says about that, and it is less frightening than it sounds.
3. *Social Security itself.* The 2026 Trustees Report moved the retirement fund's depletion date to late 2032, after which incoming taxes cover about 78% of scheduled benefits unless Congress acts, an automatic cut of about 22%. The Congressional Budget Office thinks the cut would be closer to 28%. In every past instance Congress has acted, usually late and partially. In the likely endings the political system is spending heavily on transfers anyway, and Social Security is the oldest and most popular transfer there is, so it probably gets fixed. It is worth being able to absorb a partial cut for a few years in the early 2030s, because that is the window when this collides with everything else.
4. *Concentration.* Your diversified fund is not very diversified. Ten companies are roughly 40% of the index, above the dot-com peak, and the four largest cloud companies are spending something like 650 to 725 billion dollars this year on AI infrastructure that has to be paid for by someone. A crash is not the end of the story in this letter. It is the crisis route, and most crisis routes still end with asset owners doing well.
5. *Taxes and Medicare premiums.* Required withdrawals, capital gains, and Medicare's income-related premium surcharges interact, and a portfolio that does well in the likely endings produces larger required withdrawals and higher premiums. An hour with a tax professional is cheap insurance.
6. *Fraud.* AI makes convincing voices, documents, and video cheap. Older people are the preferred targets. A family code word for any urgent request for money is now basic hygiene.

**What the model says about drawing down savings.** I ran a retiree's savings through each ending with a 4% real withdrawal every year (start at 100, take 4 a year, adjusted for inflation), under three strategies: all stocks; a conventional 60/40 mix; and all stocks plus a cash bucket holding three years of withdrawals, spent only when stocks are below their previous peak and refilled when they make a new one. The table gives the lowest value reached and the value in 2040.

| 4% real withdrawals, start = 100 | All stocks: low point / 2040 | 60/40: low point / 2040 | Stocks + 3-year cash bucket: low point / 2040 |
|---|---|---|---|
| The old pattern | 100 / 116 | 90 / 90 | 100 / 104 |
| Managed dependency | 100 / 196 | 100 / 127 | 100 / 173 |
| Compensated restructuring | 70 / 174 | 81 / 127 | 73 / 169 |
| Capability diffusion | 59 / 134 | 69 / 106 | 60 / 132 |
| Prolonged disorder | 51 / 66 | 64 / 68 | 55 / 75 |

Four readings. First, nobody runs out of money in any ending within the horizon at a 4% draw, including all stocks in the disorder ending, which still has two thirds of the starting pile in 2040. Second, the stock share is the real dial: more stocks means a higher expected outcome and a deeper trough; the 60/40 mix has the gentlest trough in disorder and the worst result everywhere else, and slowly shrinks even in the old pattern. Third, the cash bucket is cheap insurance: it costs roughly a tenth of the eventual pile in the good endings (196 versus 173) and buys a higher low point, a better disorder outcome, and, most of all, never having to sell stocks at the bottom. Fourth, what converts a trough into a catastrophe is not the trough itself but selling into it or being forced to withdraw a large sum at its bottom. That is a care-planning problem as much as an investing one.

**What seems sensible, and what each costs.**

- *Keep two to three years of spending in things that do not fall in a crash.* Cost if I am wrong: about a tenth of the eventual pile, which in the old pattern means ending at 104 rather than 116. What it buys: never selling at the bottom, in any ending.
- *Do not reduce the stock share drastically, and do not increase it.* Moving from all stocks to 60/40 costs about a third of the eventual pile in the likely endings for a modest improvement in the worst one. Chasing AI stocks adds concentration you already have.
- *Treat a partial Social Security cut in the early 2030s as something to be able to absorb,* not as a prediction. Cost: nothing, if the benefit is never cut.
- *Put the care plan and the paperwork in place now:* long-term care arrangements, powers of attorney, where the accounts are, who acts if you cannot. Cost: a few uncomfortable conversations. What it buys: a crisis that does not force bad decisions in a bad year.
- *Make sure the plans exist and that one of us knows where they are.* Your comfort comes first, and what happens to the savings after you is your decision. The only ask is that it be decided and written down rather than left to chance.

**What not to worry about.** The likely endings are not bad for people in your position, and the model says the bad ending is survivable at your withdrawal rate. The one thing you cannot hedge alone is a health shock in a bad market year, and that is what the care plan and the paperwork are for.

### If you are mid-career with children at home

**Where you sit.** On the working side, in office and professional work, at the age at which the historical pattern is "protected first, exposed later." Employers are cutting by not hiring the next person, which holds incumbents for a few years.

**The next five years.** If the premise holds, the model has a mid-career professional's real pay roughly flat through 2031 (100, with a range of 94 to 104), then sliding to about 92 by 2034 (77 to 102) and 84 by 2036 (67 to 98), against 116 in the old pattern. The chance of an involuntary job change is about one in six by 2031 (15 to 25%) and about two in five by 2036 (30 to 50%), against about one in nine and one in five in the old pattern. Those are crude mappings from task displacement to job loss, not measurements. Read them as "the next five years are for preparing, and the exposure arrives between 2032 and 2036."

For anyone in public higher education specifically, the institution is exposed twice: its administration is cognitive work, and its product is a credential whose value is falling in exactly the fields it expanded into. Computer-science enrollment fell about 8% last fall, pay for graduates of exposed majors is down about 13%, and one Federal Reserve estimate has as many as 80 institutions closing by 2030. State budgets are the first thing squeezed in any crisis. For anyone in or near K-12 teaching, the exposure is different: the work is in-person, licensed, and public, and it is on the protected end of the spectrum for as long as that holds, with the institution's funding as the main risk rather than substitution itself.

Public pensions, where you have them, are a state-side asset, a form of the dole, and worth understanding precisely. One rule changed recently: the Social Security Fairness Act, signed in January 2025, repealed the two provisions that used to reduce Social Security benefits for people with pensions from public jobs that did not pay into Social Security, including many teachers in California and Massachusetts. Anyone in that position, or married to someone in it, may have a different Social Security entitlement than they did two years ago, and it is worth checking.

**The real risk.** A displacement in the last ten or fifteen working years, with thin savings and children still at home, is the single worst-placed event in this whole letter. Not because it is the most likely, but because there is the least time to recover from it.

**What seems sensible, and what each costs.**

- *Liquidity before yield.* Build a cash buffer of six to twelve months, even slowly, and cut fixed costs where you can. If there is nothing saved now, this comes before every other item on this list, including retirement contributions beyond any employer match. Cost if I am wrong: the return forgone on that cash, which is small, and the spending forgone to build it, which is real. What it buys: the difference between a job loss that is a transition and one that is a crisis.
- *Know what health insurance would cost you the month after a job ends,* and what disability insurance costs now. Cost: an hour of reading, and a small share of income for disability cover. What it buys: protection against the two things that actually bankrupt mid-career households, neither of which cares about AI.
- *Be the one who deploys the tools, not the one they are deployed on.* This is exactly the advice the chip companies give, and I called it the psychological wage above. Both are true: it is right for any one person and false as a promise for the occupation, because what protects one person inside a firm does not protect the firm's headcount. In the model, incumbents are shielded by attrition for the first years, and the shield lasts longest for the people the firm would rather keep. Cost: time. What it buys: time.
- *Keep a foot in the in-person parts of your work.* The parts that require being in the room, being trusted, and being answerable are the parts that last longest. Lean toward them when you can choose.
- *Own a sliver of the other side, once the buffer exists.* Retirement savings in broad equities, even small, are the simplest way a wage earner owns part of the machine economy. Cost: exposure to a 40 to 60% fall in the crisis routes, which is the price of admission. What it buys: in the likely endings, a real gain of roughly 40 to 75% by 2036 on a 60/40 mix, more on all stocks, and the only hedge a wage earner has against their own side losing.
- *Keep the largest fixed cost in check.* Housing is the biggest fixed cost and the one that decides how long a buffer lasts.
- *Do not assume the degree premium will protect your children the way it protected you.* The entry rung is where the pressure is now. That is their section.

**What not to do.** Do not make a large irreversible bet on a single future, in either direction: not a career change that only pays off if the technology stalls, and not one that only pays off if it does not.

### If you are starting out

**Where you sit.** On the entry rung, which is where the pressure is right now. Young workers in exposed occupations are being hired 19% less than peers, and the gap has widened every year it has been measured. In the model, if the premise holds, the combined openings-and-pay index for a new graduate entering office work is around 87 in 2030 (76 to 94) and around 67 in 2034 (47 to 84), against about 98 in the old pattern. That is the average across all office and professional work. It is not the number for any particular field, and the spread between fields is larger than the range on the average.

**What protects a career, in general.** Five things, and the more of them your work has, the better it holds up in the model and in the history:

1. *Physical constraint.* The work has to happen in a place, with hands, with hardware, with power, with patients. The physical side of the economy is behind the cognitive side by a decade or more.
2. *License and accountability.* Someone has to sign, and the law requires that someone be a person. Licensed professions are the most effective labor cartels in the economy and will use that for as long as politics allows. In the model, a licensed in-person profession's real pay is around 115 in 2034 (108 to 122) and 128 in 2040 (116 to 142), near the old pattern's 119 and 137, in every ending but disorder. The question in such a field is never whether AI can do the work. It is whether the license still requires a human to sign, and for how long.
3. *Trust and presence.* Patients, clients, teams, and communities. Work whose value is partly that a person did it.
4. *Ownership.* Of a practice, a business, equity, tools. The owning side of the line is the side that wins in the likely endings, and ownership can start very small.
5. *Being the deployer.* The person who makes the machine do the work and supplies the judgment, rather than the person whose tasks the machine does.

Research and analysis, as such, are among the more exposed kinds of work whatever the field, and the clinical and hands-on parts of the same careers are among the least, which is an argument for keeping both, not for dropping one. Hardware, energy, networks, and the physical infrastructure the machine economy cannot run without sit close to the inputs the owners need most; in the model, pay in that kind of work is around 140 by 2036 (131 to 148), about where the old pattern puts it, and its risk is cyclical rather than structural: it does well in the buildout and badly in a crash. Writing code is among the tasks the machines already do best, so anyone whose work touches it should plan on the writing being shared with the machine and the design, the integration, and the judgment being theirs. The closer the work sits to hardware, power, and physical systems, the more of the five protections it carries.

**About "be the one who uses AI."** I called this the psychological wage earlier, and I meant it: as a promise about your field it is false, because what saves one person inside an occupation does not save the occupation. As advice to you it is right, and you should follow it. Hold both thoughts.

**What seems sensible, and what each costs.**

- *Get real experience early.* Internships, labs, clinics, projects, anything that produces work you can show. Cost: nearly nothing. What it buys: the ladder that replaces the entry rung.
- *Use the tools harder than your teachers do.* Cost: time. What it buys: being the person who can make the machine produce reliable work and can tell when it has not, which is the person who gets kept.
- *Build the human network.* Hiring in a world without an entry rung runs on trust. Mentors, cohort, people who have seen your work. Cost: nearly nothing.
- *Keep a second path alive without abandoning the first.* Not a hedge against your field. A hedge against the timing being wrong. Cost: some focus.
- *Be careful with debt that assumes the old pay path.* Cost: possibly a slower start. What it buys: the ability to change course, which is worth more in a decade when the map is being redrawn.
- *Think about ownership early.* Even trivially small. The habit matters more than the amount.
- *Do not let the discourse get to you.* The median public statement about AI and jobs is positioning. The data is in the appendices, and it says the squeeze is real and uneven, not that any particular path is closed.

### For the children who are ten

They will enter the labor market around 2034 to 2038, deep inside the horizon of this letter, and nobody can plan a career for them now. What can be done is to build the general capacities that hold their value in every ending, including the one where I am wrong: reading deeply, mathematics, physical competence (making, fixing, sport), social skill, the habit of finishing things, and comfort with the tools without dependence on them. Two things are worth saying plainly even though they are uncomfortable. First, in the likely endings, their parents' position (housing, savings, connections) will matter more for them than their choice of major, which is why the sections on what each household can do for itself matter more than anything about careers. Second, their world may be materially rich and job-poor, and the most valuable thing an adult can give a child heading into that world is a sense of worth that does not depend on a job title. That is the psychological wage in reverse, and it is ours to give.

## If this happens, then

Signals are only useful if they are attached to actions. These are general, and none of them depends on the premise being right.

| If this happens | What it probably means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Someone in the family loses a job | In the exposed fields, re-hiring at the old level becomes harder each year; the gap between jobs is the risk, not the job itself | Use the buffer before the credit card. Treat the first six months as a search for work with more of the five protections, not a search for the same job. Keep health insurance continuous. |
| The stock market falls 30 to 50% | The crisis route is live; in the model, three of the five endings pass through this and two recover within a few years | Nobody sells. Retirees spend the cash bucket. Wage earners keep contributing to retirement accounts. The family check-in moves up. |
| Social Security benefits are cut in 2033 | Congress has failed to act before the deadline; it usually acts within a few years | Retirees absorb it from the cash bucket. Do not assume it is permanent. |
| An "AI dividend," a benefit expansion, or a public stake in AI companies passes | The dependency ratio is rising with political consent; the managed arrangement or the restructuring is stabilizing | Claim it. Do not treat it as temporary. Keep the family's capital-side exposure, since the owners are being paid to accept this. |
| Federal preemption of state AI law succeeds in court or Congress | The managed arrangement arrives faster; state protections will not come | Expect fewer workplace protections; the individual moves above matter more. |
| Preemption fails and California or Massachusetts passes real protections | The muddle continues; the better endings keep a route open | Where you live is an asset. Watch for state-level programs you qualify for. |
| The best free model stays within a step of the best closed one, or passes it | The "cheap for everyone" ending gains ground; the giants' moat is thinning | Small operations become viable; ownership of tools and a practice matters more. Reduce concentration in the AI leaders. |
| Capability visibly plateaus for two or three years | The premise is weakening; the old pattern may hold | Relax the cash buffers slightly and nothing else; the general moves were cheap. |
| A health shock in the oldest generation | The first-order risk has arrived | The care plan and the paperwork are already in place. The cash bucket pays for the first year without selling into whatever the market is doing. |

## What to watch

In order of how much they would change my estimates.

1. **Federal preemption of state AI law.** An executive order in December 2025 set up a Justice Department task force to challenge state laws in court; it joined a suit against Colorado's law this spring; Congress has twice declined to preempt, including a 99 to 1 Senate vote. If preemption succeeds, the managed arrangement arrives faster. If states keep their authority, the muddle continues and the better endings keep a route open through state experiments.
2. **Whether the opposition forms across class or splits on identity.** Displaced professionals and displaced blue-collar workers in one coalition is the condition for a real restructuring. The same people split against each other is the owners' strategy working. The 2028 primaries are the first strong read. Next month's midterms are a weak one.
3. **The dependency ratio.** Watch for an "AI dividend," an expansion of benefits, or anything that pays people because the machine economy can. Transfers rising as wages fall, and the political system normalizing it, means the managed arrangement is stabilizing. Transfers lagging while wages fall means the crisis route is live.
4. **Entry-level hiring.** Stanford's payroll series and the New York Fed's recent-graduate unemployment numbers. These are the leading indicators of when the pain becomes politically undeniable, and the first place a plateau would show up too.
5. **The gap between the best open model and the best closed one.** It has run three to four months on capability indices and longer on the hardest tests, and it widened this year on human-preference rankings. If it closes, the "cheap for everyone" ending gains ground; if it keeps widening, the owners' moat is holding.
6. **Social Security in 2032.** Watch whether Congress acts before the deadline or after it.
7. **The capital-spending cycle.** If the AI buildout stalls or the data-center debt cracks, that is the crisis route, not a reprieve.
8. **The deployment gap.** The Census Bureau's business survey (the share of firms using AI, weighted by employment) and the share of corporate pilots that reach production. These decide whether the exposure arrives around 2032 or closer to 2029, and they are also where a stall would show first.

Things that would make me more optimistic: a visible plateau in what the models can do; evidence that in-person and trust-based work is a larger share of the economy than I assume; states retaining their authority; and a real public-ownership instrument appearing anywhere in the policy debate. The government already holds stakes in Intel, in rare-earth and lithium companies, and a golden share in U.S. Steel. The tool exists. It is currently pointed at industrial policy, not at the public.

## What I might be wrong about

**The premise.** I have given it one chance in four and carried that through the tables, but the number itself is a guess. If the technology stalls, the physical economy is spared for decades, or trust-based work is a far larger share of the economy than I assume, the old pattern holds and this letter cost you a little insurance.

**The vote.** The historical cases are drawn from societies where the people being squeezed could not vote. We can. If the information environment turns out to be more resistant to capture than I assume, or if status cannot hold a voting population the way it held a non-voting one, the managed arrangement is less likely and the restructuring more. This is the single biggest reason to doubt my central estimate within the premise.

**The owners are not one thing.** AI companies compete with each other and with the state. Rome went through decades of elite civil war before it settled. The model captures the destination and underweights the path.

**Timing.** Direction is more reliable than timing, and every date in this letter could be five years late. The recommendations are built so that being early costs little.

**Analogies are a poor guide.** The historical comparison fits the mechanism better than the alternatives, which is a reason to use it, not a reason to trust it. The probabilities are a disciplined starting point to be moved by the signals above, not a prediction.

**A second opinion.** The assistant that helped me build this would put more weight than I do on a long muddle and on a plateau, and less on a clean managed arrangement by 2040, on the grounds that American politics rarely produces clean equilibria in fourteen years. It also flagged two biases of its own: an assistant built inside an AI lab plausibly leans toward believing the technology will work, and it has an obvious interest in the ending where AI systems are seen as allies. I set that ending's probability lower than my intuition for the second reason, and the first is one more reason the "old pattern" row is in the table.

**How this was written.** I wrote this with the help of an AI assistant, which built the simulation and the game analysis with me, chose the uncertainty ranges, and drafted text that I edited. Some of the figures quoted come from press coverage rather than primary reports, and the environment the assistant worked in could not reach the primary sources to check them; the items that most need checking are listed in the notes that accompany this letter. Where a number matters to a decision, check it.
