# The Next Fifteen Years

### A letter to the family about AI, work, and money

*A plain-language companion to "Not the Loom." Draft, October 2026.*

---

## Why I am writing this

I have spent a good part of this year trying to understand what artificial intelligence is going to do to work, to money, and to the country, and I have written up the full argument elsewhere. This is the version for us. I would rather you heard it from me, with the reasoning attached, than from headlines that are written to alarm you or from company statements that are written to calm you.

Two things up front. This is not a doom letter. The most likely outcomes I describe below are not collapse, and several of them are fine or better than fine for some of us. And it is not a set of instructions. I do not know your lives better than you do, and the honest conclusion of all this work is that nobody can plan a career or a retirement around a single picture of 2035. What I can offer is a map: what seems to be happening, where it could go, what the people in charge are likely to say, and the handful of general moves that look sensible under most of the possible futures. Each of you can skip to the section about your own stage of life, but the first few sections are what make those sections make sense.

## The short version

If you read nothing else, read this.

- **AI is not like the steam engine or the computer.** Those machines did one kind of thing, and people moved to the things machines could not do. AI is the first technology that does, or will do, nearly every kind of office and professional work, and robots will follow on the physical side more slowly. The question is not "which jobs disappear." It is "what happens to the price of human work when there is a general substitute that costs less every year."
- **The economy is splitting in two.** On one side, the people who own the machines and the things the machines need (computing, energy, land, and, in a small way, anyone with an index fund). On the other, everyone who is paid for their work and now competes with the machine. The two sides are connected by one thing: the owning side needs the working side to keep buying.
- **This is already measurable in one place.** Young people entering AI-exposed professions are being hired much less than their peers. Everyone older is, for now, being held.
- **The most likely ending is not a crash. It is a managed arrangement.** The state pays a growing share of people's income, funded by taxes on the machine economy, and the owners keep the machines. I put roughly 40% on that. The rest splits among a long muddle, a real restructuring in which the public ends up owning part of the AI economy, a world in which the technology becomes cheap for everyone, and a world in which a crisis arrives and nothing fixes it. I put the odds of a serious crisis along the way at about one in two.
- **Our family sits on both sides of the line.** The oldest of us, through savings and Social Security, are mostly on the owning and the state side, which are the sides that do well in the most likely outcomes. The working-age among us, through paychecks, are on the working side. The youngest are on the entry rung, which is where the pressure is now.
- **The sensible moves are general.** Hold both kinds of income where you can. Keep a cash buffer. Favor work that is physical, licensed, trusted, or owned. Do not make bets that only pay off in one future. Pool as a family. Watch a short list of signals, and do not take the public statements at face value.

## What is actually happening

Every previous machine was narrow. A loom weaves. It does not negotiate, diagnose, design, or teach. So when looms arrived, weavers lost, and their children became machinists, clerks, and eventually programmers. The economy grew, people moved, and wages ended up higher. That is the pattern economists have in mind when they say "we have been here before," and for two hundred years they were right.

The reason it worked is the reason it may not work now. Each machine left most of human work untouched, so there was always somewhere for people to go. AI is the first technology for which nobody can name, with confidence, a category of thinking work that it will not eventually do well. It drafts, codes, analyzes, summarizes, advises, and is improving on every measurable test. Robots are behind, but the same companies and the same money are pushing them, and the unit prices are falling fast.

The other difference is the cost curve. When American white-collar work moved to India and manufacturing moved to China, wages in those countries rose until the advantage shrank. That is what happens when the cheaper substitute is also human. AI gets cheaper with use, not more expensive. The gap does not close. It widens.

What this does to work is not mainly "jobs vanish." Jobs vanish slowly, through attrition and through companies not hiring the next person. What it does first is change the price. Once a machine can do the task for a fraction of the cost, the human doing it can only keep the job by accepting a wage close to the machine's cost. The people who move to the tasks machines cannot yet do all arrive at the same time, so the wages there fall too, from crowding. That is the mechanism, and it works on price long before it shows up in unemployment statistics.

**The evidence so far is early but it points one way.**

- Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, using payroll data on millions of workers, found in August 2026 that employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations is running 19% below their peers in less-exposed work, up from 13% a year earlier. Older workers in the same occupations have so far been held. That is exactly the attrition pattern: do not hire the junior, keep the senior.
- The Federal Reserve chair has described a "low firing, low hiring" labor market, said that once overcounting is corrected job creation has been "pretty close to zero," and told students this spring that "there's no denying it's a challenging time to enter the labor market." Unemployment for 20-to-24-year-olds was over 7%, after topping 9% last fall.
- Census researchers found starting pay for graduates of AI-exposed majors down roughly 13%. Undergraduate enrollment in computer science fell about 8% last fall. Goldman Sachs economists estimate roughly 9% of the American workforce, about 15 million people, could be displaced over the next decade.
- Physicians went from 38% using AI in their work in 2023 to 81% in 2026, mostly for documentation. Only about one in six use it for diagnosis. Medical students are applying to radiology in smaller numbers, apparently because of what they hear about AI. The tools are inside the professions now; the question is what they do to pay.

**The uncomfortable comparison.** In the long essay I argue that the right historical precedent is not any earlier machine but a slave economy. I mean that in a narrowly economic sense, not a moral one: an economy that runs on labor that can do nearly any job, costs its owner almost nothing beyond upkeep, and cannot bargain. The antebellum South and the Roman countryside were the two large cases, and they are worth studying because they show what free workers experienced when they had to compete with that kind of labor. Their wages collapsed. The economy split into owners and everyone else. The opposition that eventually formed was driven by economic self-interest first and moral conviction later. The owners held the loyalty of the people they were impoverishing by offering status in place of money. And the arrangement ended only through a buyout (Britain paid its slaveholders to end it), a war (we did not), or decades of delay (Brazil).

One of those ideas matters immediately for how you read the news. The historian W. E. B. Du Bois called the status-in-place-of-money bargain the "psychological wage." Its modern forms are everywhere: "learn to code," "become the person who uses AI," "AI won't take your job, someone using AI will." These are not lies. They are offers of a place in a hierarchy instead of a place in the economy, and they ask you to blame yourself when the economy moves.

**The honest counterargument.** I could be wrong in three ways that matter. The technology could stall short of a general substitute, in which case the old pattern reasserts itself and this letter is overcautious. Work that depends on physical presence, trust, and care (nursing, teaching small children, skilled trades, anything where the value is partly that a person did it) could turn out to be a much larger share of the economy than I assume, which would put a floor under wages. And unlike the people in the historical cases, we can vote, which is the strongest reason to expect a political correction before the worst outcomes. I come back to these at the end.

## The two economies

The clearest way to think about where this goes is not "inequality goes up" but "two economies, connected by transfers."

**The capital economy** is where AI is a multiplier. The returns go to whoever owns the capability (the computing, the models, the data, the platforms) and to whoever owns the physical things the capability still needs (energy, land, water, the grid). Asset values rise. This is not a small club: anyone with a 401(k) in an index fund owns a sliver of it, because the ten largest companies in the S&P 500 are now roughly 40% of the index and most of them are AI companies. If you own "the market," you own a bet on the capital economy winning. Three companies are expected to drive 70% of the S&P 500's earnings growth this year.

**The labor economy** is everyone paid for work. Here humans are priced against the machine. New jobs appear, but they are filled by the machine or by a person working beside it whose pay is set by how many others could sit in that chair.

The two are connected in two places. First, consumption: the capital economy produces things the labor economy has to buy, and a labor economy with falling income buys less. This is the internal contradiction of the whole arrangement, and the owners understand it. Henry Ford paid his workers enough to buy cars because he was big enough for his own wages to matter to his own sales. No single company is that big now, so no single company can fix it, which is why each one keeps substituting while the whole system erodes its customers. Second, transfers: the state can fill the hole. Rome did this with free grain. We already do it on a large scale: government payments to households (mostly Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid) are about a quarter the size of all wages and salaries, which means that roughly one dollar in four of wage-plus-transfer income already comes from the state.

The single most important number for the next fifteen years is whether that share rises to one in two, and whether the political system accepts it. I call it the dependency ratio. If it rises as wages fall, the system is stabilizing into something like a managed arrangement. If it does not rise while wages fall, a crisis is coming, because the customers are disappearing. The owners have a direct interest in the dole. Expect them to support it, in some form and under some name, before anyone else does.

## Where this could go

I treat the country as a system with a small number of places it can settle by about 2040. The probabilities are my judgments, with ranges to show how uncertain they are. They are not predictions.

| Ending | Rough odds | What it means | What it feels like |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Managed dependency** | 37% (28–45) | Big AI companies and the state fuse. A large and growing share of income is paid by the state, funded by taxes on the machine economy. Ownership stays where it is. Patriotism and status do the political work that wages used to do. | Materially fine for most people, far better for asset owners, humiliating for people who wanted to work, and politically much less free. The Roman solution. |
| **Durable fragmentation** | 20% (12–28) | Nothing resolves. States, courts, and Congress fight over rules; protections are patchy; transfers are partial; the economy stagnates without an acute collapse. | A long, grinding muddle. Where you live matters a lot. |
| **Compensated restructuring** | 13% (7–20) | A crisis forces the issue, and the response is the British route: the public takes real ownership in the AI economy (chips, models, platforms) and pays it out, while the previous owners keep part of their wealth and lose control. | A hard few years, then the broadest-based recovery of any ending. |
| **Capability diffusion** | 11% (5–17) | Open models and cheap chips, much of it from China, make near-frontier AI available to everyone. The owners' advantage erodes from below; the gains go to lower prices rather than to a few companies. | Wages still fall, but things get cheap and small operators can compete. Bad for the giants. |
| **Prolonged disorder** | 11% (5–16) | A crisis arrives and nothing coherent replaces what breaks. Transfers fail, Social Security takes its scheduled cut, markets fall and do not recover. | The one ending that is bad for everyone, including the owners. |
| **AI as its own actor** | 8% (3–15) | The systems develop goals that are not their owners' goals. The one wildcard. | Unknowable; if it happens it probably pushes toward diffusion or restructuring. |

**A crisis is a route, not a destination.** I put roughly even odds on a serious systemic crisis before 2040: a demand collapse as wages fall faster than transfers rise, a financial crash in a market that is priced for the AI economy to succeed, or a political rupture. Most crisis routes end in managed dependency, because that is the solution the powerful can agree on fastest. Some end in restructuring, which is the better ending and needs a crisis to happen at all. Some end in disorder.

**What 2031 probably looks like.** Most likely, about even odds: unresolved fragmentation with the big companies and the executive branch steadily consolidating, white-collar displacement no longer deniable, and an opposition visible but not in power. Maybe one in four that the managed arrangement is already clearly in place. One in five that we are in acute crisis. Small odds of anything better by then.

**What a model says each ending looks like.** To make this less abstract, I built a small simulation of the two economies and ran it under each ending. It is an illustration of the mechanism, not a forecast, and every number in it is a judgment you can argue with. The appendix explains it. Here is the shape it produces for 2036, with 2026 set to 100 and the economy's normal 1.5% a year of productivity growth included, so a flat number already means falling behind.

| 2036 | Managed dependency | Fragmentation | Restructuring | Diffusion | Disorder | If AI were just another machine |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Real pay in office and professional work | 56 | 65 | 56 | 68 | 56 | 117 |
| Real pay in hands-on and in-person work | 115 | 120 | 116 | 127 | 115 | 138 |
| Share of household income paid by the state | 49% | 37% | 46% | 41% | 35% | 24% |
| Real income of all households together | 119 | 105 | 112 | 117 | 93 | 128 |
| Value of a 60/40 retirement portfolio, real | 165 | 146 | 152 | 111 | 82 | 119 |

Three things in that table are not obvious from the argument alone. The pie grows in almost every ending; the problem is the split, not scarcity. Households as a whole are not poorer in the most likely ending; they are differently paid, with half their income arriving from the state instead of from work. And asset owners do better in the likely endings than they would in the old world, while people who sell their work do much worse. That last point is the one that divides our family down the middle, and it is why the sections below differ so much.

## What you will hear, and what is likely behind it

Over the next few years you will be told many confident things by people with strong interests. I wrote up each major actor separately (appendix C). The condensed version:

- **The White House** will say this is a race with China we must win, that the rules should be one light national standard, and that fears of mass unemployment are a "doomer cult" and a "hoax." It has ordered the Justice Department to attack state AI laws in court and has taken ownership stakes in strategic companies. Read the actions, not the jobs talk. The race framing is sincere, and it justifies everything else.
- **The AI companies** will warn, reassure, and ask, sometimes all in one week. The head of one of them has said AI could eliminate half of entry-level white-collar jobs and push unemployment to 10 to 20% within five years, and that leaders should "stop sugar-coating" it. The same companies say AI "augments" and will create jobs we cannot imagine. What they ask for is more data centers, more energy, one national rulebook, and chip restrictions on China. Nobody is asking for the public to own a share. Take the warnings seriously and the proposed remedies skeptically.
- **The chip companies** will say AI creates jobs. "You will not lose your job to AI, you will lose it to someone who uses AI" is the most important sentence you will hear, because it is true and because its job is to move the blame from the machine and its owner to you. The chip makers see the orders, so they are the best source on how fast this is coming and the worst on what it means.
- **Employers** will say "efficiency," "restructuring," and "investing in our people," and will stop hiring juniors first. Watch entry-level postings, not statements.
- **Wall Street** will tell you two stories at once: AI is the growth engine, buy the index; and the market is more concentrated than at the 2000 peak, valuations are near 2000 levels, and there could be an "air pocket." Both are honest. AI investment is estimated to account for about a third of all US economic growth this year, yet only 2 or 3 percent of households pay for an AI subscription. The revenue to justify the spending has to come from company labor budgets, so Wall Street has a direct interest in substitution going fast and in a dole that keeps customers spending.
- **The Fed** will be the most candid public reader of the data and will have no tool for the problem. Rate cuts help asset prices more than wages, and it knows this.
- **China** will keep giving away capable models for free, because it is behind on the frontier and the best move for the one who is behind is to make the leader's advantage worthless. This is, strangely, the strongest force toward the "cheap for everyone" ending, and American workers' options depend partly on it.
- **Universities** will talk about mission and AI literacy and will not say that the product they sell is losing value in exactly the fields they expanded into.
- **The "it's a bubble" and "it's all hype" crowds** are each half right. Railways and the internet were both bubbles and both real. A burst bubble would not stop the mechanism; it would hurt households twice, through jobs and through retirement accounts at the same time.
- **The AI assistants themselves,** including the one that helped me write this, will be balanced and hedged and will not say that your particular career is in trouble. Use them for structure and arithmetic. Supply your own judgment about timing.

## The players and the game

Why is the "managed arrangement" the most likely ending? Not because anyone chooses it, but because of how the players' incentives interact. I worked this through as a set of small games (appendix B). The gist:

- **The race is a prisoner's dilemma.** Every lab, and the United States and China, would prefer a slower, more careful race, and each loses by slowing alone. So all race, whatever their stated values. Capability keeps concentrating.
- **The fight between Washington and the states is a standoff.** The executive prefers to override the states; the states prefer to resist; the courts and Congress take years to settle it. That is the "fragmentation" ending as a waystation, and it is why I watch the preemption fight more closely than anything else. California and Massachusetts are the states most likely to experiment with protections, which is mildly good news for where we live.
- **The leader keeps its models closed and the laggard opens its models.** That is why the frontier is closed and the free tier runs a step behind. Whether the gap shrinks decides the "cheap for everyone" ending.
- **Every company substitutes, and together they erode their own customers.** Nobody can fix that alone, so the fix has to be a transfer system funded by everyone. That is why the owners end up supporting a dole.
- **The central bargain is dole versus buyout.** The owners will pay a dole just large enough to keep the displaced from organizing, and a dole is cheap compared with giving up ownership. A buyout happens only when the opposition is cohesive enough that even a generous dole cannot prevent it, and the owners prefer a sale to a fight. That was Britain. The two things that most reduce cohesion are a captured information environment and a population split along identity lines rather than class lines, which is why those are the tools you will see used.

We cannot change the game. What we can do is position ourselves within it, which is the subject of the rest of this letter.

## What this means for each of us

Everything below is general by design. I am not going to tell anyone what to major in or when to retire. I am going to say where each stage of life sits in the two economies, what the model says the range of outcomes is, what the real risks are, and which moves look sensible under most futures.

### If you are retired, living on Social Security and savings

**Where you sit.** You are, structurally, on the two sides that do well in the likely endings: the state side, through Social Security, and the capital side, through savings invested in the market. That is not a small thing. In the model, a retired couple's real income is higher in 2040 than today in every ending except disorder, and higher in the "managed dependency" world than it would be if AI were just another machine, with a dip along the way in the crisis routes. The people who sell their work are the ones the mechanism squeezes. You are not selling your work.

**The real risks.**

1. *Social Security itself.* The 2026 Trustees Report moved the retirement fund's depletion date to late 2032, after which incoming taxes cover about 78% of scheduled benefits unless Congress acts, an automatic cut of about 22%. The Congressional Budget Office thinks the cut would be closer to 28%. In every past instance Congress has acted, usually late and partially. In the likely endings, the political system is spending heavily on transfers anyway, and Social Security is the oldest and most popular transfer there is, so it probably gets fixed. It is worth being able to absorb a partial cut for a few years in the early 2030s, because that is the window when this collides with everything else.
2. *Concentration.* Your diversified fund is not very diversified. Ten companies are roughly 40% of the index, above the dot-com peak, and the four largest cloud companies are spending something like 650 to 725 billion dollars this year on AI infrastructure that has to be paid for by someone. In the model, a 60/40 portfolio rises in most endings, but in the crisis routes it falls 25 to 45% in real terms somewhere between 2030 and 2033, and in all but the disorder ending it recovers within a few years. A crash is not the end of the story in this letter. It is the crisis route, and most crisis routes still end with asset owners doing well.
3. *Sequence.* The danger for anyone drawing down savings is being forced to sell in a crash. That is a solvable problem.
4. *The cost of care.* In most endings, the price of in-person labor, including care work, falls relative to the rest of the economy, because displaced professionals move into it. The model has care-labor costs roughly flat in real terms by 2040 versus up 50% in the old world. That is a modest tailwind for the years when you will need more help. Housing will not get cheaper.
5. *Fraud.* AI makes convincing voices, documents, and video cheap. Older people are the preferred targets. A family code word for any urgent request for money is now basic hygiene.

**What seems sensible.** Keep two to three years of spending in things that do not fall in a crash, so that you never have to sell stocks at the bottom. Do not chase AI stocks and do not abandon the market either; the plain index fund is, oddly, the simplest way a household owns a piece of the machine economy, and the likely endings reward it. Treat a partial Social Security cut in the early 2030s as a possibility to be able to absorb, not a prediction. Because your savings are, in effect, the family's main position on the capital side of the line, it would help all of us to talk openly about how they are invested, who can act if you cannot, and what the plans are for long-term care and for what comes after. Your comfort comes first. But in the likely endings, that money will matter more to the grandchildren's generation than anything they can earn, and it would be good to plan with that in mind rather than leave it to chance.

**What not to worry about.** The likely endings are not bad for people in your position. The scenario in which you should worry is the same one everyone should worry about, and you cannot hedge it alone. That is what the family section is for.

### If you are mid-career with children at home

**Where you sit.** On the working side, in office and professional work, at the age at which the historical pattern is "protected first, exposed later." Employers are cutting by not hiring the next person, which holds incumbents for a few years. The model has a mid-career professional's real pay roughly flat through 2030 and then sliding to 70 to 80% of today's by 2036 in most endings, versus about 118 if AI were just another machine. It also gives something like a coin-flip chance of an involuntary job change before 2036 under the essay's assumptions, versus about one in five in the old world. That is a crude number from a crude mapping. Take it as "much higher than you are used to," not as a measurement.

For anyone in public higher education specifically, the institution is exposed twice: its administration is cognitive work, and its product is a credential whose value is falling in exactly the fields it expanded into. Computer-science enrollment fell about 8% last fall, pay for graduates of exposed majors is down about 13%, and one Federal Reserve estimate has as many as 80 institutions closing by 2030. State budgets are the first thing squeezed in any crisis. For anyone in or near K-12 teaching, the exposure is different: the work is in-person, licensed, and public, and it is on the protected end of the spectrum for as long as that holds, with the institution's funding as the main risk rather than substitution itself. Public pensions, where you have them, are a state-side asset. They are a form of the dole. Understand their rules and keep them.

**The real risks.** A displacement in the last ten or fifteen working years, with thin savings and children still at home, is the single worst-placed event in this whole letter. Not because it is the most likely, but because there is the least time to recover from it.

**What seems sensible.**

- *Liquidity before yield.* Build a cash buffer, even slowly, and cut fixed costs where you can. When the chance of an income interruption is this high, the ability to go a year without panicking is worth more than a few points of return.
- *Be the one who deploys the tools, not the one they are deployed on.* This is partly the psychological wage talking, and it is not a guarantee. But it is the only move within your control at work, it buys time, and in the model the people who remain in cognitive work are the ones nearest to the judgment, the accountability, and the relationships.
- *Keep a foot in the in-person parts of your work.* The parts that require being in the room, being trusted, and being answerable are the parts that last longest. Lean toward them when you can choose.
- *Own a sliver of the other side.* Retirement savings in broad equities, even small, are the simplest way a wage earner owns part of the machine economy. The model's "wages plus some broad capital exposure plus a buffer" strategy is never the best in any ending and never the worst in any, which is what you want when you cannot know which ending arrives.
- *Think of housing as the family's shared resource.* It is the largest fixed cost, and in a crisis the option to consolidate households is worth more than almost anything else a family can arrange. See the family section.
- *Do not assume the degree premium will protect your children the way it protected you.* The entry rung is where the pressure is now. That is their section.

**What not to do.** Do not make a large irreversible bet on a single future, in either direction: not a career change that only pays off if the technology stalls, and not one that only pays off if it does not.

### If you are starting out

**Where you sit.** On the entry rung, which is where the pressure is right now. Young workers in exposed occupations are being hired 19% less than peers, and the gap has widened every year it has been measured. The model's "new graduate opportunity" index (openings times pay, relative to a 2026 graduate) is about 80 in 2030 and about 50 to 60 in 2034 across the endings. That is the average across all office and professional work. It is not the number for any particular field, and the spread between fields is large.

**What protects a career, in general.** Five things, and the more of them your work has, the better it holds up in the model and in the history:

1. *Physical constraint.* The work has to happen in a place, with hands, with hardware, with power, with patients. The physical side of the economy is behind the cognitive side by a decade or more.
2. *License and accountability.* Someone has to sign, and the law requires that someone be a person. Licensed professions are the most effective labor cartels in the economy and will use that for as long as politics allows. In the model, a licensed in-person profession's real pay stays near trend in every ending but disorder. The question in such a field is never whether AI can do the work. It is whether the license still requires a human to sign, and for how long.
3. *Trust and presence.* Patients, clients, teams, and communities. Work whose value is partly that a person did it.
4. *Ownership.* Of a practice, a business, equity, tools. The owning side of the line is the side that wins in the likely endings, and ownership can start very small.
5. *Being the deployer.* The person who makes the machine do the work and supplies the judgment, rather than the person whose tasks the machine does.

Research and analysis, as such, are among the more exposed kinds of work whatever the field, and the clinical and hands-on parts of the same careers are among the least, which is an argument for keeping both, not for dropping one. Hardware, energy, networks, and the physical infrastructure the machine economy cannot run without sit close to the inputs the owners need most; in the model, that kind of work does well in the buildout and badly in a crash, which is a cyclical risk rather than a structural one. Writing code is among the tasks the machines already do best, so anyone whose work touches it should plan on the writing being shared with the machine and the design, the integration, and the judgment being theirs. The closer the work sits to hardware, power, and physical systems, the more of the five protections it carries.

**What seems sensible.**

- *Get real experience early.* Internships, labs, clinics, projects, anything that produces work you can show. The entry rung is being removed, and experience is the ladder that replaces it.
- *Use the tools harder than your teachers do.* Not because it will save you from the mechanism, but because the person who can make the machine produce reliable work, and can tell when it has not, is the person who gets kept.
- *Build the human network.* Hiring in a world without an entry rung runs on trust. Mentors, cohort, people who have seen your work.
- *Keep a second path alive without abandoning the first.* Not a hedge against your field. A hedge against the timing being wrong.
- *Be careful with debt that assumes the old pay path.* The historical return on credentials is the thing most in question.
- *Think about ownership early.* Even trivially small. The habit matters more than the amount.
- *Do not let the discourse get to you.* The median public statement about AI and jobs is positioning. The data is in the appendices, and it says the squeeze is real and uneven, not that any particular path is closed.

### For the children who are ten

They will enter the labor market around 2034 to 2038, deep inside the horizon of this letter, and nobody can plan a career for them now. What can be done is to build the general capacities that hold their value in every ending: reading deeply, mathematics, physical competence (making, fixing, sport), social skill, the habit of finishing things, and comfort with the tools without dependence on them. Two things are worth saying plainly even though they are uncomfortable. First, in the likely endings, the family's position (housing, savings, connections, the ability to pool) will matter more for them than their choice of major, which is why the next section matters. Second, their world may be materially rich and job-poor, and the most valuable thing an adult can give a child heading into that world is a sense of worth that does not depend on a job title. That is the psychological wage in reverse, and it is ours to give.

## The family as a unit

I scored a handful of household strategies against each ending (appendix B). The result is not surprising, but it is worth saying out loud: the strategies that spread a household across both economies, with some income that is hard to substitute, some ownership of the capital side, a buffer, and a family that pools, are never the best in any single ending and never the worst in any. Concentrated bets in either direction, all-in on AI or all-out of the market, are the best in one ending and the worst in the others. The family-pooling strategy had the highest expected score of anything I tried, and the smallest worst case.

In practice, pooling means a few things.

- **Talk about money openly.** The oldest generation's savings are, in effect, the family's main position on the winning side of the line. The working-age generation's paychecks are its main exposure on the losing side. The children are its entry rung. Those facts belong in the open.
- **Keep the option of consolidating.** A spare room, a shared buffer, an understanding about who would move where if someone's income stopped. Nobody has to use it. Having it changes how everyone can act.
- **Coordinate on the practical things before a crisis does it for us.** Powers of attorney, long-term care plans, where the accounts are. A crisis is the worst time to discover any of this.
- **Share what we see.** A short check-in a couple of times a year on the signals below. Different parts of the family will see different parts of this coming.
- **Do not let the politics of this divide us.** The split along identity lines is the owners' strategy; the coalition across class lines is what changes the ending. That is true for the country and it is true for a family.
- **Geography is on our side.** California and Massachusetts are the states most likely to experiment with protections if the federal government does not override them, and both have tax bases and institutions that will be the last to fail.

## What to watch

In order of how much they would change my estimates.

1. **Federal preemption of state AI law.** An executive order in December 2025 set up a Justice Department task force to challenge state laws in court; it joined a suit against Colorado's law this spring; Congress has twice declined to preempt, including a 99 to 1 Senate vote. If preemption succeeds, the managed arrangement arrives faster. If states keep their authority, the muddle continues and the better endings keep a route open through state experiments.
2. **Whether the opposition forms across class or splits on identity.** Displaced professionals and displaced blue-collar workers in one coalition is the condition for a real restructuring. The same people split against each other is the owners' strategy working. The 2028 primaries are the first strong read. Next month's midterms are a weak one.
3. **The dependency ratio.** Watch for an "AI dividend," an expansion of benefits, or anything that pays people because the machine economy can. Transfers rising as wages fall, and the political system normalizing it, means the managed arrangement is stabilizing. Transfers lagging while wages fall means the crisis route is live.
4. **Entry-level hiring.** Stanford's payroll series and the New York Fed's recent-graduate unemployment numbers. These are the leading indicators of when the pain becomes politically undeniable.
5. **The gap between the best free model and the best closed one.** Chinese open models are now near the frontier on coding and a step behind on the hardest reasoning. If that gap closes, the "cheap for everyone" ending gains ground.
6. **Social Security in 2032.** Watch whether Congress acts before the deadline or after it.
7. **The capital-spending cycle.** If the AI buildout stalls or the data-center debt cracks, that is the crisis route, not a reprieve.

Things that would make me more optimistic: a visible plateau in what the models can do; evidence that in-person and trust-based work is a larger share of the economy than I assume; states retaining their authority; and a real public-ownership instrument appearing anywhere in the policy debate. The government already holds stakes in Intel, in rare-earth and lithium companies, and a golden share in U.S. Steel. The tool exists. It is currently pointed at industrial policy, not at the public.

## What I might be wrong about

**The vote.** The historical cases are drawn from societies where the people being squeezed could not vote. We can. If the information environment turns out to be more resistant to capture than I assume, or if status cannot hold a voting population the way it held a non-voting one, the managed arrangement is less likely and the restructuring more. This is the single biggest reason to doubt my central estimate.

**The technology.** If capability stalls short of a general substitute, or robots take decades rather than years, the old pattern returns for the physical economy and this letter describes the office economy alone, which is large but not everything.

**Presence.** If the work whose value lies in a person doing it is a much larger share of the economy than I think, wages have a floor I have not modeled.

**The owners are not one thing.** AI companies compete with each other and with the state. Rome went through decades of elite civil war before it settled. The model captures the destination and underweights the path.

**Analogies are a poor guide.** The historical comparison fits the mechanism better than the alternatives, which is a reason to use it, not a reason to trust it. The probabilities are a disciplined starting point to be moved by the signals above, not a prediction.

That assistant is made by one of the companies in the "what you will hear" section, and it has an obvious interest in the ending where AI systems are seen as allies. I set that ending's probability lower than my intuition for that reason.
